THERAPY SERVICES
Therapy is not easily described in general statements. It varies depending on the personality of both the counselor and the client and the particular concerns the client wishes to address. It is not like visiting a medical doctor because it requires a very active role on your part. In order to be most successful, you will have to work hard both during and between sessions.
Therapy has both benefits and risks. Risks sometimes include experiencing uncomfortable feelings like sadness, guilt, anxiety, anger, frustration, loneliness, and helplessness. Therapy may include remembering uncomfortable experiences or doing things in a new way, both of which can be challenging. Therapy has also been demonstrated to have benefits for those who undertake it. It often leads to a significant reduction of feelings of distress, better relationships, and resolution of specific problems. There are, however, no guarantees about what will happen.

CONFIDENTIALITY
In general, the confidentiality of all communications between a client and a mental health professional is protected by law, and your therapist can release to others information about your care only with your written permission. However, there are a number of exceptions.
In most judicial proceedings, you have the right to prevent your therapist from providing information about your treatment. However, in some circumstances, such as child custody proceedings and proceedings in which your emotional condition is an important element, a judge may require our testimony if the judge determines that resolution of the issue demands it.
There are some situations in which we are legally required to take action to protect others from harm even though that requires revealing some information about a client's treatment. If there is evidence a child, an elderly person, or a disabled person is being abused, we must file a report with the appropriate state agency. If you reveal information that constitutes misconduct of any health professional, we are required to report the accused party to the state board of health; however your name will not be included in the report unless you agree to it.
If a therapist believes that a client is threatening serious bodily harm to another, we are required to take protective action, which may include notifying the potential victim, notifying the police, or seeking appropriate hospitalization of the client. If a client threatens self-harm, we may be required to seek hospitalization for the client, or to contact family members or others who can help provide protection.
These incidents are relatively infrequent. Should such a situation occur, your therapist would make every effort to fully discuss the situation with the client before taking any action.
Your therapist may provide an educational component to your services and, as such, may use examples previous clients have provided. When such an example is used, your therapist will never reveal any identifying information about the client. If the example is unique enough that a client could be identified from the example itself, the example will not be used. The work in which you engage with your therapist may yield additional examples that may be used in therapy or consulting with other clients. In this case, your personal information or any other information that could create a connection to you will never be shared.
In certain situations, your therapist may find it helpful to consult with other professionals regarding a case. In these consultations, every effort is made to avoid revealing the identity of the client. The consultant is, of course, also legally bound to keep the information confidential.
While this written summary of exceptions to confidentiality should prove helpful in informing you about potential problems, it is important that you discuss any questions or concerns you may have at the next meeting with your therapist. As you might suspect, the laws governing these issues are quite complex. While it may be beneficial to discuss how legal implications may impact you, specific legal advice should be sought from a qualified legal professional.
If you request, information may be provided with relevant portions or summaries of the applicable state laws governing these issues, as well as applicable ethical codes from the National Board of Certified Counselors.
TREATMENT OF MINORS
If you are under eighteen years of age, please be aware that the law does provide your parents with the right to examine your treatment records. It is our policy to request an agreement from parents limiting access to your records. If they agree, your therapist will provide them only with general information on how your treatment is proceeding unless your therapist believes that you are in some danger, in which case your parents will be notified of the concern. Your therapist will also provide them with a summary of your treatment when it is complete upon their request. Before giving them any information, your therapist will discuss the matter with you and will try to resolve any objections you may have about what is to be disclosed.
The standards of our profession require consent from both parents prior to engaging in therapy. In the event parents are not living together, written consent is required from both parents. If it is not possible to obtain written consent from the other parent, the court ordered parenting plan regarding custody and medical decision-making will be required. In some cases, attestation of attempt to contact or inability to contact will suffice. A therapist may elect not to provide services to a minor if both parents do not agree to the services. In such a case, you may wish to contact a counselor who specializes in high-conflict parenting situations. For more information visit https://azpa.org/Family-Court-Roster
